How Do Tax Brackets Work? A $1,000 Raise, $170 More Income Tax

The short answer

Only the dollars above each bracket line pay the higher rate. A $1,000 raise that crosses into the 22% bracket adds just $170 of federal income tax in our 2026 example, so the person still keeps about $830 after federal income tax.

Key numbers

  • 2026 single brackets run from 10% to 37%; the standard deduction is $16,100.
  • On an $80,000 salary: taxable income $63,900, federal income tax $8,770, effective rate 11.0%, marginal rate 22%.
  • A $1,000 raise from $66,000 to $67,000: $500 at 12% ($60) + $500 at 22% ($110) = $170.
  • With Social Security and Medicare (7.65%), the raise still adds about $753.50 to pay.

Questions people ask

Will a raise put me in a higher tax bracket and lower my take-home pay?

With federal income tax brackets alone, no. Only the part of your taxable income above a bracket line is taxed at the higher rate, so a raise always adds take-home pay. Separate rules, like credits that phase out as income rises, work differently.

What is the difference between marginal and effective tax rate?

The marginal rate is the rate on your next dollar of taxable income. The effective rate here is your total federal income tax divided by your salary. On an $80,000 salary in 2026 (single, standard deduction), they are 22% and about 11%.

What are the 2026 federal tax brackets for single filers?

10% up to $12,400; 12% up to $50,400; 22% up to $105,700; 24% up to $201,775; 32% up to $256,225; 35% up to $640,600; 37% above that, per IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32.

Assumptions

Tax year 2026, single filer, wages only, $16,100 standard deduction, no credits, federal income tax only (no state or local taxes) unless stated. Social Security + Medicare = 7.65% employee share. Priya, Dana and the coworkers are fictional examples.
